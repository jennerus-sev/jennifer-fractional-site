Storytelling & digital advocacy

Bridging reflection and action.

How might narrative advocacy translate into a digital experience that helps everyday people turn personal reflection on injustice into civic action? The study drew on the Center for Story and Witness’s in-person workshop to design and test a self-guided experience.

OrganizationCenter for Story and Witness

Mixed-method research

Study what helps people move from caring about an issue to acting on it.

Trauma-informed design

Build in consent, privacy, and control over pacing.

Prototyping and testing

Test the workshop’s emotional arc and the transition to action.

The challenge

A story can move someone. What comes next?

At an in-person workshop, a facilitator can give people time to reflect, read the room, and respond when someone needs support. The Center needed a way to carry that care into a self-guided experience.

The study focused on people who care deeply about social issues and may feel unsure how to get involved. How could a digital workshop help someone connect a story to a meaningful next step?

Approach

The story of the study.

  1. 01

    Identify gaps

    Identify where people felt moved by an issue and where they struggled to act on it.

  2. 02

    Research, theory, and literature

    Use mixed-method research and theory to understand how storytelling, behavioral design, and trauma-informed practice could help.

  3. 03

    Prototype and test

    Build and test a self-guided digital workshop based on CSW’s in-person storytelling model.

  4. 04

    Findings and next steps

    Use what resonated and where people got stuck to refine the workshop and plan the next tests with CSW.

Theoretical frameworks

  1. 01

    Behavioral design

    Wendel’s five-step model for behavior change helped define a clear, achievable next action.

    Stephen Wendel (2020). Designing for Behavior Change, 2nd Edition.
  2. 02

    Narrative transportation theory

    Becoming absorbed in a story can influence beliefs and shift perspective.

    Melanie C. Green and Timothy C. Brock (2000). The Role of Transportation in the Persuasiveness of Public Narratives.
  3. 03

    Trauma-informed design

    Consent, pacing, and emotional safety shaped how people could move through the workshop.

    Megan Legawiec (2025). Trauma-informed content design: a comprehensive guide.
  4. 04

    Communication theory

    Erwin’s work on framing helped connect the invitation to participate with what people already cared about.

    Kim Erwin (2014). Communicating the New: Methods to Shape and Accelerate Innovation.

Research

Understand what would help people take part.

The study combined stakeholder input, secondary research, platform analysis, and audience research. Two check-ins with CSW connected the early research and later usability findings to the organization’s decisions.

Stakeholder interviews
CSW leadership described why narrative integrity, emotional pacing, and consent matter in a facilitated workshop.
Secondary research and platform review
A literature review and analysis of 14 storytelling and civic-action tools surfaced patterns in onboarding, trust, and the transition to action.
Audience research and survey
Early research informed three provisional personas. A survey of 27 people explored motivations, sharing preferences, and realistic ways to take action.
Stakeholder check-ins and prototype usability
A check-in helped shape the prototype direction. Six moderated sessions tested the workshop, and a later check-in turned the findings into next steps.

Research findings

What helps people move from reflection to action?

Read the full research paper ↗
Storytelling opens the door

Stories can prompt reflection, and people differ in their readiness to share. In the survey, 79% preferred talking privately with someone they trust and 46% reported sharing a story or cause on social media.

Motivation is there. The next step is unclear.

Among survey respondents, 36% wanted to act but weren’t sure where to start. Many of the tools reviewed offered storytelling or action without a clear way to move between them.

Trust and safety come first

CSW’s facilitation practice and the literature emphasized consent, privacy, and control over pacing. Those safeguards needed to be visible before people shared personal writing.

Small wins make an effort feel worthwhile

Survey responses pointed to a need for acknowledgment and a way to see what a contribution accomplished, even when the action was small or private.

Framing and feasibility drive participation

People responded to invitations grounded in what mattered to them. Talking with someone, volunteering, or donating felt more achievable to many respondents than a broad call to activism.

Prototype usability findings

The story resonated. The path forward needed more clarity.

In six moderated sessions, Miles’ video helped participants understand the workshop’s purpose. Testing showed where the experience needed more guidance, choice, and time.

Participants skipped key instructions

Some missed copy and even headings. Important guidance needed more visible cues at the moment it was relevant.

People wanted more stories to choose from

Participants asked for a library with a wider range of stories and lived experiences.

Motivations called for different paths

Participants wanted options for personal reflection, advocacy, or memoir, with next steps tied to the issue or story they chose.

The emotional tone worked. Privacy and purpose needed clearer cues.

Participants wanted repeated reassurance about what stayed private, what sharing meant, and where they were in the workshop.

A short format could open the door

Several participants doubted they could move from reflection to meaningful action in a few minutes. The feedback pointed to a need for longer, more guided options.

Roadmap and recommendations

Test demand before building the full digital workshop.

The roadmap was co-created with CSW leadership after the prototype and test findings were presented. The first proposed test was an in-person storytelling workshop to learn whether people would sign up and what they would pay.

  1. Pilot in person

    Test demand and pricing for a facilitated storytelling workshop.

  2. Try free digital prompts

    Give people a short, low-pressure way to experience the approach.

  3. Test distinct paths

    Explore separate options for reflection, advocacy, and memoir.

  4. Develop the online workshop

    Adapt the material for an expert-led format and test its usability and value.

  5. Pilot with partners

    Explore partner-hosted workshops and broader beta testing.
